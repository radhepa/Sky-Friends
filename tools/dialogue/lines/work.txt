## work.harpist
Every wind wants its own tuning. Aure likes the strings slack and sweet; Borre wants them tight as a held breath.
Before the harps wake I hold up a wet finger, feel what {wind} is doing, and tell the pegs. They grumble. Pegs always grumble.
Walkers say a wind harp plays itself. It doesn't. It plays what you let it, and the letting is my whole trade.
Harp strings come up from the walkers as plain string. Three dawns soaked in dew and stretched across the Hall's rafters, and they've learned to sing.
The Hall of Echoes has seven echoes. On a still evening you can hear all seven answer, one after another, like a family calling each other home.
Leading the Evensong is mostly raising one hand at exactly the right moment and hoping the whole village is watching it.
Sefa is the hardest wind to play under. Warm and lazy: the strings sag, the notes slur, and the whole Hall sounds half asleep by noon.
Borre snapped four strings on me this morning. There's a red welt on my wrist from the last. Worth it: you should have heard the chord just before it went.
My first season as an apprentice, I was only allowed to dust the harps. I learned more from dusting than I ever did from playing.
Never tune in the noon sun. The frames swell, and by dusk you're a hair flat and the whole Evensong knows it.
The bell says dusk is one note. My harps say it's another. The village sings somewhere in between and calls it harmony.
A {job}'s fingertips go hard as skywood bark. Touch mine. Years of strings in there.
Some days the wind plays a phrase I've never heard, and I scramble to catch it before it blows on. Half our songs were stolen from the sky like that.
A cracked frame sings a little sour forever. We never throw one out. We give it to someone a bit sour themselves, with love, and it suits them.
Don't pluck a wind harp, {player}. Stroke it, the way you'd stroke a sleeping koi. Walkers always pluck.
The children fly into the Hall to shout their names and hear them come back seven times. I pretend to scold them. I did exactly the same.
A Cloud Whale passed close by the Hall once and sang. Every harp answered at once and nobody touched a string. I'll never play anything that good.
Before a storm the harps hum by themselves, low and nervous. I drape them in veilcloth, the way you'd cover a birdcage at night.
My teacher could tune by smell. "Peaches on the wind: that's Sefa, loosen the fourth string." Never wrong, not once.
The hardest part of leading the Evensong is stopping it. Everyone wants one more verse. The day's already down; let it rest.
Every harp in the Hall has a name. The great harp at the back of the stage is Old Gust. The little one by the door is Hiccup, and you'll know why the moment you pass it.
A sliver of amethyst set in a harp's bridge holds the tuning true for days. The skymerchant buys it off walkers for us, fair winds to them.
Sometimes I take a small harp down to the Cloud Garden and play for the koi. The gardeners say it settles them. I suspect the koi are being polite.
If you see me hovering by a harp with my eyes shut, I'm working. Truly. A harpist listens ten times for every note played.
The stage in the Hall is left open to the sky on purpose. The wind has to fly straight through, or the harps go dumb.
Vell is the harper's wind. A note on Vell carries clear across {village} and down through the clouds. Some nights I'm sure the bell-villages below can hear us.
My tuning pegs are skywood, turned from fallen branches. I've worn them smooth as peach stones.
Old harp strings never go to waste. The lamplighter twists them into wicks. I try not to think of my best notes burning.
My apprentice once tuned every harp to Borre on an Aure morning. The dawn sounded like two neighbours quarrelling. We still tease about it.
Truth be told, I can't hold a tune with my own voice. My hands do the singing for me.
When someone goes up past the blue, we play the Long Unwinding: every string loosened, one by one, until the harp falls quiet. It's the hardest piece we know.
Tune at dawn, play the morning in, mend strings by noon, teach the children, nap, lead the Evensong. The nap is the part I guard most fiercely.
The weavers say my harps are too loud for combing. I say their looms clack off the beat. We've agreed to disagree, loudly.
How does the wind know the tune? It doesn't. The wind brings the breath. We built the throat.
I've led more Evensongs than I can count, and my chest still flutters when the whole village comes in on the first note.
Wind a gold thread round the lowest string and it purrs. A trick from {village}'s first harpist. We don't share it with other villages. Well. Now I've shared it with you.
When a walker plays a wind harp, every Cirri near comes to listen. That isn't only politeness. We're checking your tuning.
A little tuning bar of gilded skystone hangs round my neck on a ribbon. Tap it on a knuckle and it gives the true note. I'd be lost without it.
There's a chord we only play at a Lengthening, as the new long veils unfurl. It makes even the bellkeeper cry.
After dark we play softly and leave the big harps covered. The old harpists said music draws phantoms, and nobody's brave enough to test it.
Borre alone on the harps, no hands at all, sounds like something old and grieving. I let it play. Some winds need to be heard out.
I don't play for praise. I play so the wind has somewhere to go. Praise is lovely, though. You may.

## work.weaver
We fly up before Aure is properly awake, while the high wisps are still damp, and draw the cirrus out in long strands with our combs. Dry cloud tears. Dewy cloud stretches.
A veil comb has three hundred teeth of skywood, each split finer than a hair. My teacher's comb is older than the Loom House itself.
Spinning cloud is mostly patience. You roll it between finger and thumb until it remembers it would like to be a thread.
The blue at a veil's root isn't dye. It's the colour cirrus keeps when you take it from the very top of the sky. And the rose at the tips is dawn, caught in the fraying.
Gold runs down each edge of a veil. Not for show, whatever the skymerchant tells walkers. Gold keeps the edges from unravelling in Borre.
Most of my work is mending. A torn veil comes in, its owner trying hard not to cry, and I lay phantom membrane over the tear and stitch it down with string, small as I can.
Phantom membrane is the only patch that takes to cloud. Slice it thin, soak it overnight in rainwater, and it turns as clear as a moth's wing.
Weaving a child's long veils for the Lengthening takes a whole moon. I hum their name into every row. They never know. They fly straighter for it.
Veilcloth for awnings and cushions is easy: wide combs, loose weave, done by noon. Veils are another thing. One slipped thread and someone flies lopsided for a year.
The loom keeps a beat, clack and hush, clack and hush. My apprentice dozes off to it. So do I, some afternoons.
A walker once tied my best sky veilcloth round their shoulders and jumped off the Lift Dock to see if it would fly them. The windkeeper caught them, and my veilcloth, in that order.
The Cirrus Veil you walkers buy is a little cousin of ours: short, sturdy, forgiving. It won't fly you, but it will let you down gently.
Rose veilcloth takes longest. You may only comb the clouds that blush at sunrise, and they blush for about as long as it takes to say so.
My fingers are cold all year round. Cloud keeps the chill of the high air even indoors. Touch my hand if you don't believe me, {player}.
Fire stays out of the Loom House. Not a candle, not a lantern, not the lamplighter on a festival night. Especially not on a festival night.
There's a knot only weavers know, the Holdfast. Tie it right and the veil will tear before the knot gives. Tie it wrong and it slips in the first gust. No pressure.
Old veils are never thrown away. We unpick them and comb their cloud in with the new. Every veil in {village} has a little of someone's grandparent in it.
On a still day the cirrus droops and won't comb at all. "Never trust a still day." I'm certain a {job} said it first.
I can tell whose veils are whose from across the Veil Court, just by how they move. Who's tired, who's in love, who skipped breakfast. Veils tell everything.
My first veil had a pucker in it the size of a peach. My teacher flew on it for a whole year anyway, so I'd see that it could fly.
Children's veils are short so they can't fly too far. We let them out a hand's width every season, and the children measure each other constantly.
Never put a feather in a veil. Feathers belong in cushions. A veil with a feather woven in flies like a startled bird.
The best cirrus grows where a Cloud Whale has just swum through. The Old Singers comb the sky for us, in their way.
A gardener once begged a veilcloth net to shade the koi. I made it. By noon the koi had tangled themselves in it, all nine. We don't talk about it.
Every weaver has a mark, worked in near the root. Mine is three little stitches like a bird's foot. Look closely at the veils in {village}; you'll find it on half of them.
Sefa brings warm, loose cloud, good for cushions and little else. Borre's cloud is crisp as frost and makes the strongest veils in the sky, if your fingers survive the combing.
I mended a veil yesterday that had snagged on a skywood branch. Its owner swears the tree reached out for it. I sighed and said nothing about gardeners.
A loom for cloud is mostly empty space: a skywood frame, gold-wire heddles, and air. Cloud weaves best with room to breathe.
When I finish a pair of veils I hang them in the window for a night, so they can learn the wind before anyone flies on them.
Walkers buy veilcloth by the bolt and make carpets of it. Carpets. For walking on. I try very hard not to think about it.
The Loom House windows are always open, and weaving is quiet work. I hear every bit of gossip that floats past. I'm the best-informed Cirri in {village}, I'm told. Mostly by me.
My hands know the patterns so well I can weave and daydream together. Some of my best veils I don't remember making.
Never cut a veil-thread with a blade. Break it with your teeth, gently, like biting into a peach. Metal makes a veil remember the ground.
My apprentice has lovely hands and no patience whatsoever. I was the same. Patience grows. Hands don't.
A walker once asked me to mend their elytra. Stiff, strange wings. I patched them with membrane and string, and they flew. I hummed for a week.
The Lengthening is my favourite day and my most frightening. Every eye in the village is on the veils I made. If they flutter wrong, I'll feel it for a month.
Walkers' string comes in every thickness. I sort it by feel: fine for mending, middling for hems, thick for the harpists, who'd take it all if you let them.
Cloud gets tired. A veil worn every day for years needs fresh combing each spring, the way a cushion wants plumping.
How do I know a morning's good for combing? The dew sits on my eyelashes. If it doesn't, I go back to sleep.
"A Cirri is only as high as their veil is whole." Weavers hear it at every mending. It's flattering. It's also a great deal to carry.
I wove the rose hangings in the Veil Court. When the evening light comes through them the whole court goes pink, and I pretend not to watch people noticing.
Walkers' gold nuggets are beaten thin as breath and drawn through a pierced skystone to make veil-thread. My arms ache just telling you about it.

## work.gardener
Grafting is the heart of the work. Slip a twig from a good old peach into a young skywood's bark, bind it with string, and wait. If the tree agrees, you'll have peaches by next spring.
The koi know my veils. When I fly over the pool they rise to meet me like little silver clouds. Anyone else, they scatter. I may be a touch smug about that.
Now and then a Cloud Koi sheds a Sky Scale. I find them floating at the pool's edge in the mornings and keep a pouch of them for whoever looks like they need luck.
A sky peach is ready when it smells like Sefa. Pick it a day early and it's hard as skystone; a day late and the bees have beaten you to it.
The garden's roots hang down through the bottom of the island. Once a moon I fly underneath to check them. It's dark and drippy under there, and I love it.
Peach blossom opens with Aure. If Borre takes the sky that week, I fly round wrapping every bud in veilcloth like a baby. Every one. Hundreds.
Every handful of soil in the Cloud Garden was carried up from far below by the first Cirri, a pouch at a time. We guard it like gold. Better than gold.
Koi are fussy eaters. Crumbs of peach, a pinch of pollen, a glow berry if they've been good. Never melon. They spit melon.
I count the koi every morning. Nine yesterday, nine today. One hides under the lily pads and I count it twice for luck.
Bees came up with the first gardeners, the story goes, in a hive lashed to someone's veils. Their great-great-grandchildren still work my peach trees.
Skywood leaves go gold at the edges when they're thirsty. I catch rain in veilcloth funnels and carry it round in a gourd, tree by tree.
Walkers think gardening is digging. I've never dug a thing in my life. I coax. There's a difference.
The weavers would like every cloud in the sky for combing. I need a few left alone to shade the koi at noon. We have a gentle arrangement, mostly made of glaring.
Any tree that grows too heavy at the island's edge gets pruned, however lovely. We all know what happened to the Belltowers.
Flowers in the Cloud Garden are chosen for the bees, the koi and the Evensong, in that order. Bluets for the bees, lilies of the valley for the dusk, pink tulips because the children ask.
Our saplings go down with walkers through the skymerchant. Somewhere below, a skywood is growing on the ground and wondering why it feels so heavy.
My hands smell of peach and pond water, always. There are worse things to smell of.
We split the bark for grafting with a sliver of amethyst, never a blade. It's cleaner, and the tree forgives it faster.
A koi went missing last spring. I found it three islets over, swimming happily round the Lantern Spire in the rain. They follow the wet.
Throwing peach stones off the edge is a crime in my garden. Somewhere down there a walker takes one on the head. I once heard the yelp all the way up.
The children help with the peach harvest. One for the basket, one for the mouth. I've learned to plan for half.
Koi sleep with their eyes open. My first season as a {job} I thought they were all ill, and sat up three nights singing to them.
Glow berries grow on the undersides of the islands, among the hanging roots. I pick them upside down. It is not dignified.
When an old skywood dies, we never burn it. We let it break down slowly and the garden drinks it back. The harpists get the best branches for pegs.
A Cloud Koi outlives any of us. The oldest in our pool was here when my teacher's teacher was learning. It looks at me like I'm new.
I plant peach stones along the islet edges. Their roots hold the soil when Borre comes looking to take it.
Every dawn bell, every dusk bell, every storm peal: the koi dive. I've asked for a softer bell. I was told the bell is the bell.
In a good peach year you can smell {village} from three islets off. Walkers come up the Wind Stone just following their noses.
I'm grafting a tree with three peaches on one trunk: gold, rose, and a little white one nobody's named. Taste one when they ripen and I'll name it after you, {player}.
Moss on the pool stones keeps the water sweet. Scrape it off and the koi go pale and sullen. Leave it, and they shine.
Seeds blow up from the ground on Sefa's warm breath and land in my beds: thistle, dandelion, once a whole pumpkin vine. I pull them gently and send them home.
Rot in a peach tree spreads like a rumour. One brown spot at dawn, a whole branch by the Evensong. So I check the bark every single morning.
Stand on the bridge, not over the koi pool, if you don't mind. It isn't you. It's your feet. They've never seen feet.
The trees all have names. The crooked old one by the pool is Patience. The young one that drops its fruit early is Fidget.
Half of gardening up here is gardening. The other half is stopping it all blowing away. I've pinned whole flowerbeds down with skywood pegs before a Borre night.
Walkers ask if peaches grow on the ground too. They do, I tell them. Very slowly. And very heavily.
Sky Scales come off when the koi are happy, I tell the children. Maybe they come off when the koi are itchy. Either way, they're lucky.
Pollen gets everywhere in spring: my hair, my veils, the creases of my eyelids. I sneeze gold for a month.
The best hour in the garden is just after the Evensong. The koi glimmer, the peach leaves whisper, and nobody needs a thing from me.
My apprentice once planted a sapling upside down. It grew anyway, crooked and stubborn, and it's the best tree in the garden now.
Every morning I skim the pool with a veilcloth net to clear the fallen leaves. The koi try to swim into it. I'm fairly sure it's a game.
Some walkers ask if the koi are for eating. I simply hover there and let the silence answer.

## work.bellkeeper
The bell rope is braided from forty strands of walkers' string and three of gold. It's longer than me with my veils out, and my hands know every knot in it.
Dawn bell, dusk bell, storm peal. Three songs, that's all I play. The harpists find this hilarious. The village gets up all the same.
Ringing at dawn means waking before dawn, and that means sleeping before the Evensong ends. I've never once heard its last verse.
Our bell is as thin as we could make it and as light as we dared. The old belltowers hung iron bells as heavy as whales, and you know where those villages are now.
A storm peal is long and uneven on purpose: ring, ring, pause, ring. It's not music. It's a warning, and everyone in {village} knows it in their bones.
All day I watch for storms. Borre sharpens, the high wisps vanish, the koi dive early and the chimes go quiet. When all four happen, I ring.
Ring the storm peal too early and they'll call you a worrier. Ring it too late and someone's caught over the edge in lightning. I'd rather be called a worrier.
Every dawn gets a notch in the beam above the bell. This world has seen {day} dawns. I've rung in a fair few of them myself.
The pagoda has three roofs and a chime under every eave. When all of them ring at once, the wind can't make up its mind.
I've the strongest arms in {village}. Ask anyone. Actually, don't ask the weavers.
Children sneak up the pagoda to touch the bell. I let them: one finger, very gently, and they feel the hum all the way to their elbows. Their faces!
Dusk is the harder bell. You ring it gently, so the day knows it may go. Dawn you ring bright: up, up, the sky is waiting.
Never ring the bell for fun. I did once, as an apprentice, and the whole village flew out to the Veil Court still sleep-curled, sure a storm was coming.
Bell first, then lanterns: that's the dusk rule. The lamplighter has been early twice this moon. I have noticed.
Every fifth dawn I polish the bell with a pad of veilcloth and a dab of beeswax. It keeps the note sweet. Please don't lick it.
A bell's voice changes with the weather. In snow it rings higher and thinner; on Sefa's warm days it goes round and lazy. I change my pull to match.
I'm told the bell-villages below let anyone pull their bells. Anyone! Up here only the {job} touches the rope.
Keeping time is more than ringing. Is it noon yet? How many dawns to the Lengthening? When did the last whale pass? I'm the village's memory, with a rope attached.
When someone goes up past the blue, the bell rings once, softly, at noon, and then not again till dusk. That silence is the loudest thing I do all year.
A thread of gold runs across the pagoda floor. When the bell's shadow lies on it, it's noon, and I give a little tap for anyone listening.
They say the rope chooses the next bellkeeper. It chose me by catching round my ankle when I was small and swinging me clear across the pagoda.
There's only ever one bellkeeper. It's a little lonely. No one else wakes in the dark listening for the hour.
A good dawn bell sets the koi rising, the harps humming and the peach blossoms opening. Or so I like to think. The gardeners say the blossoms manage on their own.
I dropped the clapper off the edge once while I was oiling it. It's somewhere on the ground. I like to think a walker found it and is still wondering.
There's a crack in the bell, hair-thin, near the lip. Older than me. It gives the dusk bell a little catch, like someone about to cry. I wouldn't mend it for anything.
The most I've ever rung: three storm peals in three nights. My palms were raw for a week. Nobody was caught out. I'd do it again tomorrow.
Before you ask, {player}: no, you may not ring it. Yes, I know you'd do it gently. Everyone says that.
My favourite moment is the breath before the dawn bell. Everything still, all {village} asleep, and I'm holding the morning in my hands.
Cloud Whales answer the bell sometimes. One long, low note from very far off, just after dusk. I hold the rope still and listen until it's done.
When the bell is quiet I rest my forehead on it and hum. It hums back. We're not strange, bellkeepers. We just listen to something you can't hear.
Does the noise bother me? The bell isn't noise. Noise is a walker in iron boots coming up onto the Lift Dock.
My apprentice once rang the dusk bell in the middle of the Evensong, sure they were late. The whole village stopped singing and stared. They've never been early since.
When a baby is born, the next dawn bell has three quick little rings before it. Listen for them. It's the happiest sound in the sky.
The bell has to reach every nest, even on the far islets. I swing it toward the rim at dawn and toward the court at dusk. People swear they can hear the difference.
My knees ache before a storm, and I've never knelt on anything in my life. The bell knows, my knees know, and then Borre arrives.
Fireworks are my nightmare. A walker sets one off below, every Cirri thinks it's thunder, and every face turns to me. I had to invent an all-clear peal on the spot.
I sleep in the open top tier, curled over a cushion right under the bell. If it so much as shivers in the night, I'm awake.
Storms belong to me and the windkeeper both. I ring the peal; they fetch every walker in off the Lift Dock. We've never lost one.
Every storm gets a knot in a long cord I keep: a big knot for thunder, a small one for rain, a bead for snow. The cord's three veils long now.
The dawn bell has never once been late in my time. On the morning it is, come and check on me.
People think my work is pulling a rope. It's mostly waiting: watching the light, feeling the wind, holding still for exactly the right moment. The pull is the easy bit.
You ring from the shoulders, never the wrists, or your arms are ruined by spring. I teach the children with a peach on a string.

## work.lamplighter
I'm the only one in {village} who carries fire. Caged in gold, always, never once loose. People still fly a little wide of me at dusk. I don't mind.
My pole is skywood, three times my own length, with a gold hook at one end and a snuffing cup at the other. I could find either in the dark. I usually do.
Wick-trimming is the real craft. Too long and the flame climbs and blackens the gold. Too short and it drowns in its own wax. I trim each one to a fingernail.
The great lantern is a cluster of cages under the spire's gilded cap, with one more on the very point. Lighting them all takes exactly the Evensong's first verse.
From the top of the Lantern Spire at dusk I can see the bell-villages' lights coming on far below, little sparks in the dark. I always wave. Nobody ever waves back.
Afraid of fire? Of course I am. Every Cirri is. That's what makes me good at this. A lamplighter who isn't afraid is a lamplighter who gets careless.
Our wax comes from the gardeners' bees and our wicks from old harp strings. So every light in {village} is a little bit garden and a little bit song.
A skin of rainwater on my belt, a wet veilcloth over one shoulder. I haven't needed either in years. I'll never go a night without them.
The weavers won't let me past the Loom House door after dusk, not even with the lantern shuttered. I support this entirely.
At dawn I fly the bridges and snuff every lantern with the cup on my pole. Click, click, click. It's the quietest work in the village, and the loveliest.
Walkers cross our bridges by lantern light and never look up. If they did, they'd see me hovering under the rail with my pole, fixing their light.
A lantern that gutters is telling me something: a draught from the wrong side, the wax gone soft, a moth in the cage. It's usually the moth.
Phantoms hate the great lantern. They circle out past its light and won't come closer. That's why it burns all night, every night.
The gold on a cage has to be polished every few dawns, or the light goes yellow and sad. A scrap of veilcloth and a great deal of breath.
I make the sky lanterns the skymerchant sells you walkers: gold nuggets bent into a cage round a little flame. Keep it away from anything dry, please.
My hands smell of wax and smoke. The children wrinkle their noses at me, then beg to see the flame. Every time, both.
I set my own hem alight once, as an apprentice. I put it out in the koi pool. The koi have never forgiven me.
The bellkeeper insists my lanterns have been early. My lanterns have not been early. My lanterns have been prompt.
Lighting the great lantern in a strong Vell is like threading a needle in a sneeze. You shield the taper with your whole body and both veils, and you hold your breath.
A lamplighter's veils go singed at the tips. Look at mine: little brown freckles. The weavers keep offering to mend them. I like them. They're my years.
Every bridge in {village} has its lanterns spaced so a walker is never more than four steps from light. I paced it out myself, flying, with my feet pretending.
On the longest night of the year we light every lantern in the village together, and the whole island glows like a peach held up to the sun.
They say you can see our great lantern from the next village on a clear night. Some nights I swear theirs blinks back. Lamplighters keep each other company like that.
Nobody cooks. I know that. But I once held a peach over a lantern to see what would happen. It went brown and smelled wonderful and I felt terribly guilty.
Snuffing is better than blowing. Blow a lantern out and the smoke goes everywhere. Snuff it and it just sighs and stops, like a child falling asleep.
My apprentice isn't allowed the taper yet. They carry the wax, the wicks, the snuffer. Maybe next spring. Fire can wait for a steady hand.
A cracked pane is the worst thing I can find on my rounds. Wind gets in, the flame leans, the gold heats. I carry spare panes in a pouch and fit them with shaking fingers.
Most Cirri fear fire loudly. Mine is a quiet fear, more like respect. Fire is a guest you never turn your back on.
Lantern light makes the koi glow on the bottom of the pool. The gardeners say it unsettles them. I say they're showing off.
Why not glow berries in every cage? I tried. A cage of glow berries is pretty and faint, and walkers fall off bridges in faint.
There's a ladder inside the Lantern Spire, just for walkers. I've never used it, but I keep it polished. Want to come up and watch the great lantern lit, {player}?
I measure the night in candle-stubs. Tall at dusk, a stub by dawn. When I change them I know to a breath how long the dark has been.
The lantern stage wears a crust of old wax a hand thick, like old snow. Every spring I scrape it off and roll it into new candles between my palms.
When someone goes up past the blue, I leave the lantern by their nest unlit for one night. The dark gap in the lights says it better than words.
Fireworks? I'd ground anyone who brought one near my lanterns. Well. I'd want to.
Court first, then the near bridges, then the islets one by one, and the great lantern last as the Evensong rises. I could do the rounds with my eyes shut. I don't.
When the wind's wrong I light the great lantern from right in among the cages, curled under the gilded cap like a moth. Don't tell the weavers I fit.
Walkers carry torches! Raw flame on a stick, no cage at all, waved about like nothing. I want to scold them, and also ask how they're so brave.
A good {job} is never noticed. If nobody remembers the lanterns, they were lit right.
I love the moment the great lantern catches. One cage, then the next, then the whole cluster, and the gold throws light on every veil in the Veil Court. Everyone looks up, just for a breath.
Glowing candles stand in the nest windows too. I trim them for the old ones who can't fly the rounds any more, and they pay me in honey and stories.
Wicks are twisted from three strands: one for dusk, one for midnight, one for dawn, my teacher said. It isn't true. I still say it while I twist.

## work.skymerchant
Phantom membrane is the dearest thing a walker can bring me. It's the only patch that takes to a torn veil. Every membrane you sell me keeps a Cirri flying.
Feet planted, that's how walkers haggle. I haggle drifting slowly backwards. It works wonders. They always follow.
Fresh stock every dawn: peaches from the garden, veilcloth from the Loom House, lanterns from the spire. By noon the best of it has gone down with the early walkers.
Feathers go into nest cushions. A good cushion takes a hundred. We sleep curled in the air above them, so they must be soft enough to dream on.
Amethyst goes to the harpists and the chime-makers. It rings true. A chime with a sliver of amethyst in it sings in the faintest breath.
Truth be told, we've little use for emeralds up here. They're green and heavy, a little piece of the ground. But walkers trust them, so I keep a jar.
The Windsong Flute is my favourite sale. Play it near any Cirri and they'll stop whatever they're doing to listen. Even me. Especially me.
A walker once tried to buy the Wind Stone. The actual stone. I offered the Lift Dock and the windkeeper as well, and watched them slowly work out I was teasing.
Wind harps don't fit in walkers' pockets, I've learned. They try. Oh, how they try.
